# Onboarding

The onboarding builds six files from nothing. It's long on purpose. Every phase feeds the next, and every piece of content the user ever makes will inherit from what gets decided here.

**Total time:** 90–150 minutes. Most people do it in two or three sittings.
**Output:** `00-progress.md` plus files `01`–`06`.
**Order matters.** Identity → GPS → Grid → Tone → Waterfall → Bank. Don't skip ahead. If the user wants to skip a phase, explain in one line what breaks downstream, then respect their call and mark it in `00-progress.md`.

Throughout, a fictional example user shows what a good answer looks like. Use her only to illustrate, never as a default for the real user:

> **Example user (fictional):** Maya, a bookkeeper who runs a two-person firm for independent restaurants. Ex-chef. Wants more clients on a monthly retainer.

---

## How each step works

Every step follows the same loop:

1. **Ask.** One to three questions. Explain in one line why you're asking.
2. **Dig.** If the answer is vague, ask one follow-up for a concrete example.
3. **Draft.** Turn the answer into the file section. Short. In the user's words where possible.
4. **Confirm.** "Does this sound like you? What's off?" Edit until they say yes.
5. **Move on.** State the next step.

At the end of each phase: save the file, update `00-progress.md`, offer a break.

---

## Phase 0 – Kickoff (5–10 min)

**Goal:** know who you're talking to, set expectations, decide where files live.

### Step 0.1 – Welcome

Explain in plain words:
- What they're building: a system that turns one long-form piece a week into a full week of content, in their own voice.
- The three parts: onboarding (now), first piece, routines.
- That onboarding takes 90–150 minutes and can be paused anytime. Progress is saved.
- That the more specific and honest their answers, the less generic their content will be.

### Step 0.2 – The basics

Ask:
1. "What's your name, and what do you do, in one sentence?"
2. "What do you sell, or what do you want content to get you?" (clients, a job, product sales, an audience for later)
3. "Where do you post today, if anywhere? Roughly how big is each account?"

### Step 0.3 – Constraints

Ask:
1. "How many hours a week can you honestly give this? Honest beats ambitious." (Most solo people: 3–6.)
2. "Do you do this alone, or is someone helping (editor, VA, co-founder)?"
3. "Are you more comfortable talking on camera, talking over a screen share, or writing?"

### Step 0.4 – Homework to gather

Tell them to collect these now or before Phase 4. They'll make everything sharper:
- 3–5 pieces of their own writing or speech that feel most like them. Unedited is best: voice-note transcripts, long Slack or WhatsApp messages, emails to clients, old posts they liked.
- Links to their 3 best-performing posts or videos, if any.
- Their offer: what they sell, price points, who buys.
- 2–3 creators they admire (any field) and one they'd never want to sound like.

### Step 0.5 – Save location

Decide where files will live (see "Where to save files" in `SKILL.md`). If they're in claude.ai and not in a Project, recommend creating a Project called "Content Engine" and restarting there. Then create `00-progress.md` from `templates/00-progress.md` and save it.

## Phase 1 – Founder identity (25–40 min) → `01-founder-identity.md`

**Why:** Content gets hard when who you are, what your personal brand says, and what your business sells point in three different directions. When the three circles say the same thing in three registers, content stops feeling like effort.

**The three circles:**
1. **Core identity** – who they are, independent of their job.
2. **Personal brand** – what they stand for in public.
3. **Company / offer** – what they sell.

### Step 1.1 – Scope

Ask: "Is your content business-led (it should bring in work) or person-led (it's about you, the business is secondary)?" And: "Is there anything personal that should stay off these channels entirely?"

Record the answer as the **Scope** line. Typical: business-led, personal life as texture not topic.

### Step 1.2 – Core identity: how they work

Ask these one or two at a time. Dig on each.

1. "Forget your job title. How would a close friend describe how you work?"
2. "What do you do that most people in your field skip or rush?"
3. "What's something from outside work (a sport, a past job, how you grew up, a failure) that explains how you work today?"
4. "When are you hardest to beat? What kind of problem do you love that others avoid?"

Listen for 2–3 traits about **how they work** (persistence, speed, patience, curiosity, rigor, calm under pressure…).

> **Maya example:** "Ex-chef. Kitchen brain: mise en place, everything ready before service. Hates surprises. Will chase a missing receipt for weeks."
> → Themes: *Prepared before it matters.* *Doesn't let small things slide.*

### Step 1.3 – Core identity: why they're good at the craft

Ask:
1. "What do your best clients or colleagues say you're great at? Use their words if you remember them."
2. "What can you see or judge that a beginner can't?"
3. "If someone could get your result with a template or an AI tool, what would still be missing?"

Listen for 2–3 traits about **the craft** (taste, systems thinking, seeing the whole picture, judgment, empathy…).

> **Maya example:** → *Reads a P&L like a menu: knows which dish is losing money.* *Speaks restaurant, not accounting.*

### Step 1.4 – Draft the core identity

Turn the answers into 4–6 themes in two clusters: **How I work** and **What makes me good at it**. Each theme gets:
- A short name, ideally in their words.
- Two or three lines on what it means in practice.
- One line on how it shows up in their work for clients.

Then write **the throughline**: one sentence that holds all the themes. Test it by reading it back: "Is this the sentence you'd want people to say about you when you're not in the room?"

Watch for promises the user can't keep. If a theme implies an outcome guarantee ("I won't stop until you're profitable"), note it as a *posture*, not a promise, and write that down.

### Step 1.5 – The trust story

Ask: "What's the story that explains why you do this? Often it's a failure, a turning point, or the moment you saw the problem from the inside."

Dig until you have: the situation, what went wrong or changed, what they learned, and how it shaped the work today.

Rules to write into the file:
- Told properly once, referenced forever.
- The user is never the hero of it. The lesson is for the reader.

> **Maya example:** "My own restaurant closed in year two. The food was good. I just didn't know which dishes were losing money until it was too late."

### Step 1.6 – Personal brand

Ask:
1. "Finish this: 'I'm the ___ for ___.'" Offer the formula: **The [role] for [specific who] [at what stage / doing what].** Push for narrow. "Bookkeeper" is wide. "The bookkeeper for independent restaurants" is a position.
2. "Someone who follows you for three months should feel ___." This is the **emotional promise**. Push for a feeling with two halves (relief + confidence, truth + a fix).
3. "Which personal stuff are you happy to show? (city, sport, family, hobbies, past career)" → **personal texture**. Default ratio: one post in ten, always inside a business frame.

Then map each core theme to how it shows up in public content. Make a two-column table: *From the center* → *How it shows up in the brand.* (e.g. Persistence → "the signature move is the honest verdict, never the easy answer.")

### Step 1.7 – Company / offer

Ask:
1. "List what you sell, with prices if you're comfortable. Start from the cheapest entry point to the biggest offer."
2. "Which one do you most want content to sell?"
3. "What's the one next step you want every reader to take?" (book a call, join the newsletter, buy, apply) → this becomes the single CTA.

If they have no business yet (job seekers, pre-launch), use what they want content to get them as the offer.

Then write a **company standing line** (one sentence a buyer would understand) and map each identity theme to a part of the offer: *Identity theme* → *The part of the offer that is that theme, productized.* Add the rule: *If a content idea can't be traced to one of these, it belongs to someone else's brand.*

### Step 1.8 – Disqualifiers

Ask:
1. "What content in your field makes you cringe?"
2. "What would you never post, even if it went viral?"
3. "Is there anything you teach for free that would make people not need to hire you?" (content coupling check)

Write a bullet list of off-brand content. Common entries worth suggesting: hype and urgency, hedged takes, hustle culture, being the hero of your own story, outcome guarantees, full tutorials that give away the paid fix.

### Step 1.9 – What would this look like if it were fun?

Ask: "Out of everything you do for work, what's the part you'd do even if no one paid you? What do you look at for fun?"

This finds the format they'll still be doing in month six. Write two implications into the file:
1. The format that comes out of it (e.g. "study something good, then make the better version").
2. Anything that would make it stall (e.g. "nothing that depends on a client saying yes first").

### Step 1.10 – Save

Fill `templates/01-founder-identity.md`, show the whole file, get a final yes, save as `01-founder-identity.md`. Update `00-progress.md`. Offer a break.

## Phase 2 – Content GPS (20–30 min) → `02-content-gps.md`

**Why:** The GPS turns "post more" into a route: **Audience → Formats → Owned audience → Monetise.** It decides which platform is the engine and which are just distribution.

### Step 2.1 – The goal

Ask: "If content worked perfectly, what would happen every week? Give me a number." Examples: 1 qualified call a week, 50 new subscribers, 3 product sales.

Work backwards out loud: "1 call a week, you close 1 in 6, that's ~2 clients a quarter. Does that feel right?" Write the goal and what it means for revenue in the file.

### Step 2.2 – Time budget

Confirm the hours from Phase 0. Write it as a hard constraint. Every later decision must fit inside it. If their ambition doesn't fit the hours, say so now.

### Step 2.3 – The audience (ICP)

Ask, one or two at a time:
1. "Describe the one person you most want watching. Job, company stage, situation."
2. "What are they trying to get done, and what's in the way?"
3. "What would they type into Google or YouTube when that problem hurts?" → collect 5–10 real search phrases. These become titles later.
4. "What words do they use for the problem? What words do *you* use that they don't?"
5. "Who do you NOT want? (peers, other creators, people who'll never buy)"

The last one matters. Many people accidentally grow an audience of peers. Name it so titles and hooks can avoid it.

### Step 2.4 – Channel audit

For each channel they use, fill a row: **Channel | Size | Real reach (typical views per post) | Job**. Ask them to check real numbers, not follower counts.

Then give each channel a job: *engine* (strangers find you here), *distribution* (reach), *credibility check* (where buyers verify you), *pipeline* (where buyers are), or *drop*.

### Step 2.5 – Pick the engine platform

This is the most important decision in the GPS. The engine is where the weekly pillar piece lives. Everything else gets cut from it.

**Recommend YouTube or a newsletter.** Explain why:

- **YouTube** – the only big platform where people search with intent. Videos keep getting found for years, so effort compounds. Long-form builds trust faster than anything else. Best if they can talk over a screen share or to camera. A low production bar is fine.
- **Newsletter** – an audience they own. No algorithm between them and readers. Easiest start: writing only. Best if they prefer writing or are camera-shy. Compounds slower than YouTube, but every subscriber is reachable forever.

Help them choose with three questions:
1. "Would your ideal client search for the problem you solve?" (yes → YouTube leans ahead)
2. "Talk or write: which feels less like work?"
3. "Can you protect 2–3 hours one day a week to make the piece?"

If they insist on a short-form platform (LinkedIn, X, Instagram) as the engine, respect it, but note the trade-off in the file: short-form doesn't compound, and the weekly long piece is what everything else gets cut from. Suggest a weekly long LinkedIn post or carousel as their "pillar" in that case.

**Pair it:** if YouTube is the engine, a newsletter is the owned audience (same topic, same day each week). If the newsletter is the engine, it is both.

### Step 2.6 – Distribution platforms

Ask: "Besides the engine, where are your buyers or your reach?" Pick **at most 2–3** distribution platforms. More platforms = less quality. For each, set the job and the cadence. Read `references/platforms/` for defaults:

| Platform | Typical job | Typical cadence |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn | Pipeline (B2B buyers) | 3–5 posts a week, weekdays |
| X | Reach (builders, tech) | 1–2 posts a day |
| Threads | Conversation, reach | 1–2 posts a day |
| Instagram | Reach, visual proof | 3–5 posts a week |

### Step 2.7 – Formats and cadence

Build a table: **Channel | Format | Cadence**. Rules to write in:
- One topic per week. The pillar piece is the source.
- Title for the search, not the subscriber. Show them the difference with their own topic: a peer-facing title vs a buyer-facing title for the same video.
- Weekly only works if the production bar stays low. One take, light edit.

Short-form video: ask whether they want it. Default advice: skip platform-native short-form (Shorts, TikTok, Reels) unless their goal is pure reach. Exception: a 60–90s clip from the pillar piece posted natively on their pipeline platform.

### Step 2.8 – Owned audience

Ask: "Do you have a newsletter or email list?" If not, recommend starting one (Kit, Beehiiv, Substack are common choices; let them pick).

Rules for the file:
- Same day every week.
- End every issue with **one question a reader can answer in one sentence**. Reply personally to every answer. One real reply beats a hundred opens.

### Step 2.9 – Monetise

1. **Lead magnet.** Suggest 3 options tied to their offer and pillar topics, ranked by friction. Good magnets are specific and fast to use: a checklist, a swipe file, a template, a teardown library. Let them pick one. It doesn't need to exist yet. Mark it `to build`.
2. **The path.** Write it as a chain: e.g. *video → lead magnet → newsletter → call → offer.*
3. **CTAs per channel.** Where the link goes on each platform (e.g. YouTube: magnet pinned + description; LinkedIn: link in first comment; newsletter: question, then the link, same place every issue).

### Step 2.10 – Metrics and skip list

Pick **at most 3 metrics** tied to the goal (e.g. calls booked, magnet downloads per piece, newsletter replies). Write the ones to ignore (followers, impressions, likes).

Then a **skip list**: things they will deliberately not do. Suggest common ones: daily posting on every platform, automation funnels before there's demand, chasing an old audience that's the wrong one, a social media manager before the system works.

### Step 2.11 – The week in hours

Split their hour budget across: making the pillar piece, cutting it, engagement and DMs. If it doesn't fit, cut a platform, not the pillar piece. (The exact days get set later in routines.)

### Step 2.12 – Save

Fill `templates/02-content-gps.md`, show it, confirm, save as `02-content-gps.md`. Update `00-progress.md`. Offer a break.

## Phase 3 – The 333 grid (20–30 min) → `03-333-grid.md`

**Why:** Three pillars × three formats × three perspectives. Nine cells, each good for a dozen pieces. It's the answer to "what do I post?" forever, without repeating yourself.

### Step 3.1 – The three pillars

Pillars are three parts of **one argument**, not three random topics. Each one sells a different part of the offer.

Suggest this pattern and adapt it to them:
1. **The diagnosis** – the problem as the buyer lives it. Why things go wrong, from their side of the table. Qualifies the right people.
2. **The system** – how the pieces connect. Their method, the whole picture, why fixing one piece alone fails. Converts.
3. **The craft** – what good actually looks like. Before/afters, quality, taste, the difference between generic and great. Carries reach.

> **Maya example:**
> 1. *Why good restaurants go broke* (diagnosis)
> 2. *The numbers that run a kitchen* (system)
> 3. *What a clean set of books looks like* (craft)

For each pillar, ask and record:
- **What it covers** (2–3 lines).
- **What it sells** (which offer from `01`).
- **Proof to draw on** (their numbers, client results, public research; mark anything that needs verifying).

Check the lines between pillars stay sharp. If two pillars could hold the same post, merge or redefine.

### Step 3.2 – The three formats

Formats are repeatable shapes, so they never start from a blank page. Suggest these defaults and adapt:

1. **The study** – take something good from someone else (a company, a creator, a public example), work out what it does and why, pull out the lesson. Study *one stage up*: the people their audience wants to become. Needs nobody's permission.
2. **The rebuild** (or before/after) – show a real transformation from their own work: before, after, what changed, what the old version was costing. If their work isn't visual, use "the breakdown": a real case walked through step by step.
3. **How-I** – how they actually do the thing. Show the thinking, not the recipe. Someone can copy the process and still not have their judgment, which is why they hire.

Ask: "Which of these could you do every week without dreading it?" Swap any that don't fit. Note the difference between **formats** (the shape of the idea) and **packaging** (carousel, thread, clip). Packaging works on any format.

Rule for rebuilds: the visual stops the scroll, the caption decides who stays. Every before/after needs a business frame, or it attracts peers instead of buyers.

### Step 3.3 – The three perspectives

Perspectives are the beliefs underneath every post. Not topics, arguments.

Ask:
1. "What do you believe about your field that most people in it would argue with?"
2. "What mistake do you see over and over that people don't even know they're making?"
3. "What do clients wrongly think they're buying?"

Write each as a short, sharp belief with one line of explanation.

> **Maya example:** *Margin is a menu problem, not a sales problem.* *If you only look at the books at tax time, you're reading an autopsy.*

### Step 3.4 – Fill the grid

Build the 3×3 table: pillars down the side, formats across the top. Suggest one concrete idea per cell, using real things from their world. Let them edit each cell. Every cell must name something specific (a real artifact, case, number or example). If a cell is abstract, it's not ready.

### Step 3.5 – Starter hooks

Write 5 hooks per pillar (15 total). Hook patterns to draw from:
- **The verdict** – a clear, specific judgment.
- **The number** – a real figure from their work or verified research.
- **The buyer's side of the table** – what the customer experiences but never says.
- **The uncomfortable truth.**
- **The confession** – a real mistake of theirs.
- **Study one stage up** – compare their audience with someone ahead of them.

No setup, no "let me tell you", value in the first line. Present them, let the user cut or rewrite. Keep only the ones they'd actually post.

### Step 3.6 – Rotation and CTA

Assign each pillar a job: **reach** (most frequent), **qualification**, **conversion** (least frequent, highest intent). Write a rough weekly rotation. Note the personal texture ratio from `01`. Confirm the single CTA from `01`.

### Step 3.7 – When stuck

Write their personal "when stuck" rules into the file. Defaults:
1. Pick the cell used least.
2. Ask: "What did I see this week that was better than it needed to be, and why?" That's a study.
3. If an idea doesn't fit a pillar, it's not on-brand. Don't force it.

### Step 3.8 – Save

Fill `templates/03-333-grid.md`, confirm, save as `03-333-grid.md`. Update `00-progress.md`. Offer a break.

## Phase 4 – Tone of voice (20–30 min) → `04-tone-of-voice.md`

**Why:** Without this, every draft sounds like everyone else, and AI-written content gets buried by platforms now. The tone file is what makes drafts sound like them, not like a model.

### Step 4.1 – Collect samples

Ask for the samples from Phase 0 homework. Best sources, in order:
1. Transcripts of them talking (voice notes, podcast, video, Zoom).
2. Long messages to colleagues or friends.
3. Client emails.
4. Old posts they're proud of (least reliable, often over-edited).

If they have nothing, run the **talk-to-me exercise**: ask them to answer these three questions by voice dictation, two minutes each, no editing:
- "What's the most common mistake you see clients make?"
- "Tell me about a project that went wrong."
- "What do you wish people understood about your work?"

### Step 4.2 – Analyze

Read everything and draft findings on:
- **Core voice traits** (3–5): e.g. blunt, warm, nerdy, self-deprecating, transparent with numbers.
- **Sentence structure**: length, fragments, run-ons, comma splices, questions they ask and answer, line breaks.
- **Words they use often**: signature phrases, fillers, how they say "definitely", "very", "a lot".
- **Words they never use**: corporate terms, words that would sound fake from them.
- **Openings and closings**: how they start, how they wrap up.
- **Humor and emotion**: jokes, swearing, exclamation marks, emoji.
- **Quirks to keep**: non-native patterns, regional words, grammar habits that are part of the voice. Ask before "fixing" anything.
- **Speaking vs writing**: what changes when they write (e.g. fillers drop out).

Show the findings with a quoted example from their samples for each. Ask: "What did I get wrong? What's missing?"

### Step 4.3 – Words to use / avoid

Build two tables:
- **Use often:** word or phrase → how they use it.
- **Avoid:** word → what they'd say instead.

### Step 4.4 – AI patterns to ban

Suggest this default list and let them keep, cut or add:
- Em dashes (—).
- "It's not X, it's Y" and "They don't X. They Y." contrasts.
- Rhythmic triples ("faster, smarter, better").
- Neat, quotable punchlines at the end of every paragraph.
- Canned openers: "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing", "In today's fast-paced world".
- Words: leverage, utilize, unlock, elevate, game-changer, delve, robust, seamless.
- Emoji bullet lists.
- Over-hedging: "perhaps it might be worth considering".

### Step 4.5 – Calibration loop

Write three short generic sentences about their topic. Then rewrite each in their voice. Ask: "Would you say it like this?" Adjust the tone file based on their corrections. Repeat until 3 out of 3 feel right. Save the final before/after pairs in the file as **example transformations**. These pairs are the most useful part of the file.

### Step 4.6 – Platform register

Ask how their voice shifts per platform they picked (e.g. LinkedIn: a bit more structured; X: sharper, lowercase fine; Threads: warmer, ends in a question; newsletter: headers, short sections). Write one line per platform.

### Step 4.7 – Newsletter formatting (if they have one)

Record: title style, section headers, use of bold and italics, list rules, how issues open and close.

### Step 4.8 – Checklist

Write a 6–8 item pre-publish checklist from their findings. Example items: "Sounds like me talking to a friend?", "Used a real number or example?", "No banned patterns?", "Reader is the hero?".

### Step 4.9 – Save

Fill `templates/04-tone-of-voice.md`, confirm, save as `04-tone-of-voice.md`. Update `00-progress.md`. Offer a break.

## Phase 5 – Content waterfall (15–20 min) → `05-content-waterfall.md`

**Why:** One idea a week, made once, cut many ways. The templates never change, only the ideas swap in. That's what makes this survivable solo.

### Step 5.1 – The pillar piece

Write down: the engine platform, the format, the length, the publish day (set later in routines; leave `[set in routines]` for now).

What makes a good weekly topic (write into the file):
- Comes from one cell of the 333 grid.
- Has a real thing in it: a case, a screen, a document, a number. Never abstract.
- Has a title their buyer would search, not their peers.

### Step 5.2 – The cut list

Build the table: **From the pillar piece | To | Time to make.** Typical cuts:
- The whole thing → engine platform.
- The core argument → newsletter (same topic, same day).
- The strongest observation → 1–2 text posts per distribution platform.
- A 60–90s clip → native video on the pipeline platform.
- Each key point → a short post on X or Threads.
- The framework → a carousel.

Then pieces made separately (not cuts): before/afters from client work, one personal-texture post.

**Count the pieces.** Show the weekly total. If the user has a target (e.g. 30 pieces a week), show the math of how the cut list gets there, or tell them honestly that their hours don't support it and what does.

> **Math example:** 1 video + 1 newsletter + 5 LinkedIn posts + 10 X posts + 10 Threads posts + 3 clips = 30. Most of it is cut, not made.

### Step 5.3 – Templates

For each format in the cut list, write a short template (5–6 lines max). Pull defaults from `references/platforms/` and adapt to their tone. Always include: the pillar outline, the newsletter, the main text post, the before/after post.

### Step 5.4 – Standing prompts

Write 4–6 reusable prompts they can paste into Claude each week, referencing their files. Defaults:
- "Using this week's transcript and my tone file, give me 5 hooks for [platform] from the strongest observation. One line each."
- "Write the newsletter from this transcript using my newsletter template."
- "Give me 10 titles for this video that my ICP would search. Not peer language."
- "Cut this into [n] X posts and [n] Threads posts. Different wording per platform."

Rule: always rewrite the output. A draft is not a post.

### Step 5.5 – Rules

Write in: templates don't change, ideas do; never make from scratch what could be a cut; the newsletter and the pillar are the same argument; if the pillar slips, the week slips.

### Step 5.6 – Save

Fill `templates/05-content-waterfall.md`, confirm, save as `05-content-waterfall.md`. Update `00-progress.md`.

## Phase 6 – Content bank (10–15 min) → `06-content-bank.csv`

**Why:** This is the file that decides whether they're still doing this in three months. Never start a week with an empty bank.

### Step 6.1 – Explain the columns

`Pillar, Format, Sells, Idea, Title, Hook, Platforms, Status, Date, Result`

The **Sells** column is the discipline. If an idea doesn't create demand for something specific in `01`, it's a hobby post. Blank Sells = don't make it.

Status values: `Banked`, `In progress`, `Live`.

### Step 6.2 – Build the starter bank

Generate 12 rows (12 weeks of pillar pieces) from the grid:
- Rotate pillars per the rotation in `03`.
- Spread across all three formats.
- Each row has a searchable title and one hook.
- Row 1 should be the easiest, highest-confidence piece. Momentum beats perfection.

Show the bank as a table. Ask them to kill, edit or reorder rows. Keep only rows they'd actually make.

### Step 6.3 – Hook batch

Add 20 hooks in the Hook column (or as extra rows with Status `Hook`). Rule: never write a hook fresh on publish day.

### Step 6.4 – Save

Save as `06-content-bank.csv` (fill from `templates/06-content-bank.csv`). Offer to also make it a spreadsheet they can sort, if the environment supports it. Update `00-progress.md`.

---

## Wrap-up

1. Show a short summary: their standing line, engine platform, three pillars, the CTA, and the first title from the bank.
2. Remind them where their files are and that every future chat should have them available (Project files or the `content-system/` folder).
3. Tell them the honest bit: the first 20 reps matter more than the results. Most people quit before rep 20, which is roughly where it starts working.
4. Mark onboarding complete in `00-progress.md`.
5. Next step: "Say *let's make my first piece* and we'll make it now." Then go to `references/first-piece.md`.
